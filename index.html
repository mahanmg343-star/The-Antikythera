02 — Discovery

Pulled from a wreck off a sponge-diving island

  • 1900
    The wreck

    Sponge divers sheltering near Antikythera find a Roman-era cargo ship on the seabed, laden with bronze and marble statuary.

  • 1901
    A corroded lump

    Among the salvage, a fused mass of bronze gearwheels goes largely unnoticed for months, dismissed as unremarkable debris.

  • 1902
    Gears, seen plainly

    Archaeologist Valerios Stais notices a gear embedded in the rock, and the fragment is recognized as some kind of instrument.

  • 1974
    X-rays and a first model

    Historian Derek de Solla Price, using gamma-ray images, proposes the device tracked calendrical and astronomical cycles.

  • 2006
    The inscriptions read

    High-resolution CT scanning by an international research team recovers thousands of hidden characters of instructional text, and the gear train is finally mapped in full.

03 — How It Speaks

Three cycles, driven by one crank

A single handle on the side turned everything at once. Inside, over thirty interlocking bronze gears translated one steady turn into three independent, astronomically correct rhythms.

FRONT DIAL

Sun & Zodiac

A pointer sweeps once around the ecliptic ring per solar year, shown against a fixed 365-day Egyptian calendar — reconciled by a clever slipping gear for the extra quarter-day.

FRONT DIAL

Moon & Phase

A second pointer, geared to the moon's uneven pace across the sky, carries a small black-and-silver ball that rotates to show the correct lunar phase for any given day.

BACK DIALS

Eclipse Prediction

Two spiral dials — one tracing 235 lunar months over 19 years, the other 223 months over 18 — let the user read off likely eclipse seasons years in advance.
